PART 1 OF CORE CONTENT
What prior authorization is — and what it is not.

Prior authorization is a payer requirement that certain services be approved before they are delivered. For ABA therapy, prior authorization is not optional — Medicaid and most commercial insurance plans require it for every CPT code from 97151 through 97158. If a clinic delivers services without an active authorization, those services cannot be billed. If they are billed anyway, any payment that follows is an improper payment subject to recoupment.

Who issues prior authorizations

The payer issues prior authorization — the insurance company or Medicaid managed care organization responsible for paying the claim. The authorization is their formal approval, in writing, confirming that they have reviewed the medical necessity documentation and agreed to cover the described services for a defined period and unit count.

Authorizations are not treatment plans. They are not prescriptions. They are payer approvals — a binding statement that the payer will pay for defined services as long as the documentation supports delivery.

What prior authorization covers

Every authorization document specifies: the client name and member ID, the authorized CPT codes, the authorized number of units, the service date range, the eligible service location (home, clinic, school, community), and the eligible providers. Services delivered outside any of these parameters are not covered by that authorization — even if the authorization document is active on the date of service.

What prior authorization does not protect you from

An active authorization does not guarantee payment. It is a commitment to pay if the documentation supports the service delivered. If the session note does not meet documentation requirements — wrong elements, missing signature, unit mismatch — the claim can still be denied on a documentation basis even with a valid, active authorization in hand.

This is the distinction that separates documentation coordinators who understand billing from those who do not. Authorization is necessary but not sufficient. The documentation must hold up too.

PART 2 OF CORE CONTENT
Reading an authorization document.

Authorization documents are not standardized. Every payer uses a different format. Some are PDFs with form fields. Some are faxed letters. Some are portal exports with codes most staff have never seen. The documentation coordinator's job is to extract the same five critical elements from every format they receive.

The five critical authorization elements

  • Authorization number: The unique identifier the payer assigns. Every claim for services covered by this authorization must include this number. If it is missing or mistyped, the claim processes without an authorization match and will deny.
  • Authorized CPT codes and units: These define what services are covered and for how many units total. Units and hours are not interchangeable — a 97153 unit is 15 minutes. An authorization for 480 units covers 120 hours of service, not 480 hours.
  • Effective and expiration dates: Services delivered before the effective date or after the expiration date are not covered. The expiration date is not a suggestion — it is the hard boundary after which all services are at financial risk.
  • Eligible service locations: Home, clinic, school, community — most authorizations specify where covered services can be delivered. A session delivered at a location not listed is not covered regardless of the date or unit count.
  • Eligible providers: Some authorizations list specific provider NPIs. If a session is delivered by an RBT not listed on the authorization, the claim for that session may deny even with a valid, active authorization.

Units vs. hours: the most common misread

Time-based CPT codes like 97153 are billed in 15-minute units. When an authorization says "480 units of 97153," that is 120 hours of face-to-face technician-delivered ABA service — not 480 hours. If a documentation coordinator is tracking in hours and the authorization is in units, the first tracking error is built in from day one. Always confirm the unit definition in the authorization document and in your tracking template before the first service is delivered.

PART 3 OF CORE CONTENT
Tracking authorizations against delivered services.

Authorization tracking is the gap that generates most improper payments across every OIG audit state. The issue is not that clinics do not have authorization data — it is that no one is actively comparing what was authorized against what is being delivered in real time. By the time anyone notices a problem, 46 days and $14,720 of services have already been delivered past the authorization expiration.

Building a tracking system that works

An effective authorization tracking system does six things. First: it lists every active authorization, by client, with the authorization number, authorized CPT codes, unit totals, start and end dates, and eligible locations. Second: it updates after every service delivery, deducting units used from units authorized. Third: it calculates units remaining automatically — not as a manual subtraction but as a formula that updates when sessions are logged. Fourth: it flags when units remaining fall below a threshold (commonly 20 units or 2 weeks of services, whichever is earlier). Fifth: it flags when the authorization expiration date is within 30 days. Sixth: it shows who is responsible for submitting the renewal and what the deadline is.

When units run out before the renewal arrives

This is the scenario that generates most authorization-related improper payments. A client has 20 units remaining. The renewal request was submitted but not yet approved. The BCBA wants to continue services. What does the documentation coordinator do?

Stop the services until the authorization is approved. This is the correct answer even when it feels like the wrong answer to families and clinical staff. Delivering services without an active authorization, even when a renewal is pending, creates claims that cannot be paid legally. The only exception is a formal "pending authorization" policy in writing from the payer — which some Medicaid plans offer and most commercial plans do not. The documentation coordinator must know their payer's specific policy before services continue.

The 30-day rule

Most payers allow submission of renewal authorization requests 30 to 60 days before the current authorization expires. The documentation coordinator's job is to identify every client whose authorization expires in the next 30 days and confirm that a renewal request has been submitted with enough lead time for payer review. Payer review can take 7 to 21 business days. If a renewal is submitted with 10 days left and the payer takes 15 days to review, services have a gap. That gap is the documentation coordinator's responsibility to prevent.

PART 4 OF CORE CONTENT
Submitting initial requests and renewals.

Initial authorization requests and renewal requests are not the same process. The documentation required, the clinical justification expected, and the submission timeline differ significantly — and submitting the wrong request type, or submitting the right request type with insufficient documentation, delays approval and creates service gaps.

Initial authorization requests

An initial authorization is submitted for a new client or for a client returning after a lapse in services. The payer is evaluating medical necessity for the first time. Required documentation typically includes: a current medical diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder with the diagnosing provider's name and date, a current treatment plan developed by a qualified BCBA, an assessment report (usually a BCBA-administered functional behavior assessment) with specific measurable treatment goals, and a prescription or referral from the client's primary care physician in states that require it.

The clinical justification must explain why ABA services are medically necessary for this specific client at this specific intensity level. Generic justifications — "client has ASD and needs ABA" — are insufficient. Payers want to see the specific behaviors being targeted, the severity level, the expected outcomes, and why the requested number of hours per week is clinically appropriate.

Renewal authorization requests

A renewal is submitted for an existing client whose authorization is expiring. The payer now has a prior authorization history to evaluate. Required documentation typically includes: updated progress notes showing measurable progress toward treatment goals, an updated treatment plan reflecting current goals and reduced or maintained intensity as appropriate, the current BCBA's attestation of continued medical necessity, and in some states a new physician referral.

The key difference from an initial request: the payer wants to see that treatment is working. If a renewal shows identical goal language to the previous authorization with no documented progress, payers may request additional documentation or deny continued services. The documentation coordinator does not write clinical justifications — but the documentation coordinator should know what to flag when the renewal packet comes back from the BCBA incomplete.

What "incomplete" looks like in practice

A renewal packet is incomplete when: the progress notes cover fewer than the required period (most payers want 90 to 180 days of notes for renewal), the treatment plan is outdated (many payers require an update within 30 to 90 days of the renewal submission), the physician referral is expired, or the BCBA's signature is missing. The documentation coordinator reviews every packet before submission and kicks incomplete packets back to the clinical team. Submitting an incomplete packet does not stop the clock — but it delays approval and may result in the payer requesting additional documentation, adding another week or more to the review timeline.

PART 5 OF CORE CONTENT
Payer differences: Medicaid, commercial, and TRICARE.

Not all prior authorization processes work the same way. The documentation coordinator who manages clients across multiple payers — which describes most documentation coordinators in private practice settings — needs to understand three distinct prior authorization frameworks: Medicaid managed care, commercial insurance, and TRICARE.

Medicaid managed care

Medicaid in most states is administered through managed care organizations — private insurance companies contracted by the state to manage Medicaid benefits. This matters because it means prior authorization rules are not uniform across Medicaid clients. A client on United Healthcare's Medicaid plan in California follows United's prior authorization policies. A client on Molina Medicaid in California follows Molina's policies. The payer is the managed care organization, not the state Medicaid program, and each MCO sets its own documentation requirements, timelines, and renewal policies within state Medicaid guidelines.

Key Medicaid considerations: most Medicaid plans allow more units per authorization period than commercial plans (some allow 1,000+ units for intensive early intervention), payer portals for submitting authorizations vary by MCO, and the appeals process for Medicaid denials typically has statutory timelines that commercial plans do not.

Commercial insurance

Commercial insurance authorization processes are more variable than Medicaid in some ways and more standardized in others. BlueCross BlueShield, Aetna, Cigna, and United Healthcare each have prior authorization requirements for ABA services — but the forms, submission portals, review timelines, and clinical documentation requirements differ by plan type, by region, and sometimes by employer group plan design. What is covered under a California BlueCross PPO plan may differ from what is covered under a California BlueCross HMO plan from the same company.

Commercial authorizations typically approve fewer total units per period than Medicaid (often 400 to 600 units per 6-month authorization versus 600 to 1,200+ per year for Medicaid). Commercial plans are also more likely to require periodic "medical necessity reviews" mid-authorization — where the payer requests updated clinical documentation before the authorization period ends.

TRICARE

TRICARE is the federal healthcare program for active-duty military members and their families. TRICARE covers ABA services under a formal Applied Behavior Analysis benefit. Prior authorization for TRICARE is submitted through the TRICARE regional contractor — either Humana Military (East region) or Health Net Federal Services (West region). TRICARE requires a formal ABA treatment plan submitted to and approved by an authorized TRICARE ABA provider. The documentation requirements are specific to TRICARE and differ from both Medicaid and commercial insurance. TRICARE clients are a small percentage of most ABA clinic caseloads — but the documentation requirements are distinct enough that a documentation coordinator managing TRICARE clients needs TRICARE-specific training on the authorization process.

WORKED EXAMPLE
Reading and tracking: annotated authorization document.

Below is an authorization document annotation and two tracking scenarios — one that works and one that generates a finding. Review both before the module scenario.

ANNOTATED AUTHORIZATION DOCUMENT

Sample Authorization — Molina Healthcare Medicaid

Client: ANDERSON, JAMILA R. · Member ID: 7889204 · DOB: 03/15/2020

Auth #: MOL-2026-0048821 ← Must appear on every claim line or the claim processes without an auth match and denies

Authorized services: 97153 (480 units), 97155 (96 units) ← 97154 is NOT authorized here — a concurrent 97154 session would not be covered

Effective: 10/01/2026 · Expires: 03/31/2027 ← Any service on or after April 1 is not covered by this authorization

Service location: Clinic and home ← Community setting is NOT listed — a school session would not be covered

Eligible providers: Any enrolled Molina provider ← No NPI restriction here — but check every authorization; some list specific providers

✓ TRACKING THAT WORKS

Documentation coordinator creates a tracking row for Jamila: Auth#, 480 units 97153, 96 units 97155, effective 10/1 through 3/31. After every service week, the coordinator subtracts units delivered. At week 10, the tracker shows 80 units of 97153 remaining (480 − 400 = 80). The coordinator flags the renewal deadline and submits the renewal request on week 10 — 6 weeks before expiration. Approval arrives in 2 weeks. No gap in services.

✗ TRACKING THAT FAILS

Documentation coordinator notes the authorization in the client file but does not build a running unit tracker. Services continue through March. On March 28, the billing team submits claims and discovers the payer is showing 0 units remaining — the 480 units were depleted by week 20. Services from week 20 through the end of March were delivered without units. Recoupment demand arrives for 180 units × $32/unit = $5,760.

MODULE SCENARIO
240 units, 46 days left, and a BCBA who wants to schedule heavy.
SCENARIO

What would you do?

A clinic has a client, Marcus T., with a Medicaid authorization for 240 units of 97153 and 48 units of 97155, effective through December 31. It is November 15. The BCBA wants to schedule 30 units of 97153 per week through year end. You run the numbers: 30 units per week × 6.5 remaining weeks = 195 units needed. Current units remaining: 180 units of 97153. The BCBA's plan would exceed the remaining authorization by 15 units.

At the same time, the authorization expires December 31, and no renewal has been submitted yet. The payer typically takes 15 to 21 business days to review renewal requests. Today is November 15. December 31 is 46 days away.

The BCBA says: "We've been approved every year. Just submit the renewal and we'll figure it out." The clinic director says she doesn't want any gaps in services for Marcus.

Decision point 1: The BCBA wants to schedule 30 units per week. You have 180 units remaining. What do you tell the BCBA about the scheduling plan?

The right answer: Inform the BCBA that the authorization supports a maximum of 180 units between now and December 31 — roughly 27 units per week, not 30. Scheduling 30 units per week creates a 15-unit overage that will not be covered. Adjust the schedule to 27 units per week maximum, or identify whether any units can be redistributed from the 97155 authorization.

Decision point 2: The renewal has not been submitted. What do you do today?

The right answer: Submit the renewal authorization request today. With payer review taking 15 to 21 business days, submitting on November 15 means approval should arrive between December 8 and December 15 — giving a 2-week buffer before December 31. Any later and the clinic risks a service gap in January.

Decision point 3: What do you document about this process?

The right answer: Document the submission date, the payer confirmation number for the renewal request, the current unit count and schedule adjustment communicated to the BCBA, and the expected approval timeline. If services gap despite the timely submission, this documentation shows the clinic acted in good faith.
