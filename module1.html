REAL AUDIT SCENARIO

The ABA Documentation Standard

In 2024, a mid-sized ABA clinic in Colorado received a Medicaid post-payment audit covering 18 months of services. The clinic employed four RBTs and two BCBAs. By every visible measure, it was a functioning, well-run operation.

The auditor reviewed 97153 session notes across a sample of clients. What she found was not fraud. No one fabricated sessions. Every client was present. Every RBT showed up and did their job.

What she found was that more than half of the session notes did not contain a specific, named target program description. Notes said things like "worked on DTT targets" and "practiced communication skills." None of those phrases tell an auditor which target program from the treatment plan was addressed. None of them create a verifiable link between the session delivered and the authorized treatment plan on file.

The documentation coordinator had been with the clinic for two years. She was not negligent. She was untrained.

SECTION 1 OF 5

What Documentation Actually Is

Most people working in ABA think of session notes as a clinical record. Something a BCBA reviews to track progress. A log of what happened in the session.

That understanding is not wrong. But it is incomplete in a way that creates enormous financial risk for every clinic where it goes uncorrected.

AUDIT REALITY

In a Medicaid billing context, a session note is legal documentation of a commercial transaction. It is evidence that a service was delivered, that the person who delivered it was qualified, that the service matched what was authorized, and that the documentation meets the regulatory requirements of the payer.

When a Medicaid auditor reviews a session note, she is not asking whether it is a good clinical record. She is asking whether it constitutes sufficient evidence that a reimbursable service was delivered as billed. Those are different questions, and the answer to one does not guarantee the answer to the other.

A session note can be clinically detailed, clearly written, and genuinely useful to the BCBA, and still generate an audit finding because it is missing a required element the payer defines as necessary proof of service.

OIG CONTEXT

The HHS Office of Inspector General audited ABA Medicaid billing in Indiana, Wisconsin, Maine, and Colorado. Across all four states, every sampled review period showed at least one improper or potentially improper payment. The most common finding was not fraud, it was documentation that did not meet payer requirements.

SECTION 2 OF 5

The Documentation Chain

ABA billing works as a chain. Each link must exist and must connect to the next. When any link is broken or missing, the chain fails, and in a Medicaid audit, a broken chain means the payment for that service is not supported.

Treatment
Plan
→
Authorization
→
Session
Note
→
Billed
Claim

The Treatment Plan establishes what services have been clinically recommended. It identifies the specific target programs, goals, intervention strategies, and authorized providers.

The Authorization is the payer's approval to deliver and bill for those services. It specifies the CPT codes covered, units approved, the date range, and eligible providers.

The Session Note documents what was actually delivered. It must connect backward to the treatment plan (by naming the specific target programs) and to the authorization (by being delivered within the authorized date range).

The Billed Claim is the financial transaction submitted to the payer. The payer matches the claim against the authorization and may review the session note.

MOST COMMON CHAIN BREAKS

Session note does not name specific target programs from the treatment plan · Session delivered after authorization expiration · Billed code does not match the documented service · Session note references a target not in the current treatment plan

SECTION 3 OF 5

Required Elements by CPT Code

Each ABA CPT code has specific documentation requirements tied to what that code represents. The requirements are not interchangeable.

CodeDescriptionKey Documentation Requirement
97153Adaptive Behavior Treatment by Protocol, DirectNamed target programs + objective trial data + prompt levels + start/end times + provider and supervisor identified
97154Group Adaptive Behavior Treatment by ProtocolAll 97153 elements PLUS names and identifiers of all group members present
97155Adaptive Behavior Treatment with Protocol ModificationAll 97153 elements PLUS the specific protocol modification made AND clinical rationale AND BCBA direct participation documented
97156Family Adaptive Behavior Treatment GuidanceFamily member name and relationship + specific strategies trained + caregiver demonstration or practice + implementation plan
97157Multiple-Family Group Treatment GuidanceAll 97156 elements PLUS names of all families present
97158Group Adaptive Behavior TreatmentAll 97155 elements PLUS group member documentation
97155 OVERBILLING ALERT

97155 requires in-session protocol modification, not just BCBA presence, not supervision, specifically a modification to the treatment protocol with documented clinical reasoning. If a BCBA is present and supervising but no protocol modification occurs, the service is 97153 not 97155. This is one of the most commonly misbilled codes in ABA.

SECTION 4 OF 5

The Nine Most Common Errors

These nine errors appear repeatedly across OIG audit findings. A documentation coordinator who can identify all nine, and catch them before submission, prevents the category of financial loss that ends clinics.

  • 1
    Vague target program description
    "Worked on communication targets" is not a target program description. "Addressed Vocal Manding for Preferred Items (Target 3B)" is. The note must name the specific target from the treatment plan. This is the single most common finding across all four OIG audit states.
  • 2
    Missing or estimated start and end times
    All ABA codes are billed in 15-minute units. "Afternoon session" or "approximately 2:00 to 4:00" is not sufficient. If documented time does not support units billed, the claim is unsupported.
  • 3
    Narrative data instead of objective data
    "Client did great today" is not data. "Client achieved 85% accuracy across 20 trials with no prompting" is data. Notes that describe performance narratively cannot demonstrate treatment plan implementation occurred.
  • 4
    Session note written significantly after the service date
    Notes timestamped days after service raise credibility questions and can expand a focused audit into a full retrospective review. Best practice is same-day completion.
  • 5
    Missing technician signature
    An unsigned session note is legally incomplete regardless of its content. The signature establishes who delivered the service and attests to the accuracy of documented information.
  • 6
    Missing or inadequate supervising BCBA documentation
    97153 services require the supervising BCBA to be identified by name and credential. "Supervision provided" without a name and credential is insufficient.
  • 7
    Session note references programs not in the current treatment plan
    When a treatment plan is updated, some targets are retired. If an RBT addresses a retired target, the session note references a program not authorized under the current clinical plan. The documentation chain breaks.
  • 8
    Billing code documented on the note does not match the service delivered
    When a note documents one code and the claim is submitted under another, the discrepancy is a red flag in any audit. Verify the CPT code on the note matches the actual service delivered.
  • 9
    Undocumented behavioral incidents
    When a client engages in a significant behavioral incident during a session and that incident is not documented, the documentation is incomplete. If it happened, it belongs in the note, factually, without interpretation.

SECTION 5 OF 5

Signatures, Timestamps, and State Deadlines

Documentation requirements have three dimensions that vary: who must sign, when the note must be completed, and what the state-specific Medicaid rules require.

SIGNATURE STANDARD

Every ABA session note requires at minimum the signature of the person who delivered the service, with their printed name and credential. For services delivered by an RBT, most payers also require the supervising BCBA to be identified by name and credential. Some require a co-signature from the supervising BCBA on individual RBT notes.

Timestamps matter. Electronic health records generate system timestamps when notes are created, edited, and finalized. These are visible to auditors and are compared against the documented service time. A note whose timestamp shows it was created three days after the documented service date will be questioned regardless of content.

STATE DEADLINE VARIATION

Medicaid documentation deadline requirements are set at the state level. The range across states goes from same-day to 30 days. Most states fall in the 24-hour to 7-day range. Best practice: same-day completion regardless of payer or state. This eliminates timeliness risk entirely and produces a timestamp pattern that reflects professional documentation practices.

PART 5: MODULE SCENARIO

Documentation Coordinator: Friday Submission Review

The Situation

California ABA clinic, 48 clients, Friday claim submission deadline

You are a newly hired documentation coordinator. The clinic serves 48 active clients across three BCBAs and nine RBTs. You are starting your second week and have been asked to review last week's 97153 notes before Friday claim submission. You pull them up in PrioraCare EHR.

DECISION POINT 1
RBT Marcus Thompson submitted 12 notes. All reference "worked on Marcus's behavior programs" without naming specific targets. Times, signatures, and BCBA documentation are all present. Notes were completed within 24 hours. Do you submit?
CORRECT ACTION
Flag for Marcus to correct before Friday. The supervision and timing documentation is correct, but the missing target program names are a documentation deficiency that creates audit exposure. Submitting as-is passes the financial risk to the clinic. Contact Marcus, ask him to add specific target names and trial data, verify corrections, then submit.
DECISION POINT 2
RBT Amelia Torres submitted two notes referencing "Target 7: Requesting with an AAC Device." You pull the current treatment plan. Target 7 was discontinued in the September 1 update. Notes are otherwise complete. Can you submit?
CORRECT ACTION
Do not submit. The notes reference a target not in the current treatment plan. Even with complete documentation otherwise, the chain between the session note and the treatment plan is broken. Contact the supervising BCBA to review whether services were delivered to the current Target 7a protocol. Do not submit until the treatment plan link is verified and corrected.
DECISION POINT 3
The BCBA tells you two clients had their authorizations expire November 15. Sessions were delivered November 16, 17, and 18. Notes are complete and accurate. Do you submit the claims?
CORRECT ACTION
Do not submit. Services delivered after authorization expiration are not covered regardless of documentation quality. Hold the November 16 through 18 claims, document the authorization status issue, and work with the BCBA and billing team to pursue an emergency authorization renewal. If a retroactive renewal is approved covering those dates, submit at that time with the renewal authorization number.

SECTION A: UNIVERSAL REQUIRED ELEMENTS (all codes)
  • Date of service documented
  • Exact start time and exact end time documented
  • Client name and identifier present
  • CPT code documented on the note
  • At least one specific named target program from the current treatment plan
  • Objective data for each target program (trials, accuracy, prompt level)
  • Provider signature with printed name and credential
  • Supervising BCBA identified by printed name and credential
  • Behavioral incidents (if any) documented factually with time, description, intervention, and outcome
  • Note completed within payer deadline

  • 97154 / 97158: Group member names and identifiers documented
  • 97155: Specific protocol modification documented with clinical rationale; BCBA direct participation noted
  • 97156 / 97157: Family member names and relationships; strategies trained; caregiver demonstration; implementation plan; group members (97157 only)
⚠ If any element in Section A is missing, do not submit. Return for correction before claim submission.
