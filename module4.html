CONSEQUENCE HOOK
One letter. 47 days late. $2.1 million owed.
WHAT HAPPENED, MAINE, 2023
$2,100,000
in Medicaid recoupment, triggered by a missed records request letter

A Maine ABA clinic received a Medicaid RAC audit records request in the mail. The letter went to the billing coordinator's inbox. She was out on medical leave. No one picked it up. By the time the clinic's director discovered the letter, the 45-day response window had closed. The RAC treated the missed deadline as an inability to substantiate the claims. All 847 claims under review were presumed unsubstantiated. The recoupment demand was $2.1 million. The clinic appealed. They won some claims back. They did not get to keep all of them.

The documentation for those sessions existed. The clinical work was real. The billing was legitimate. The clinic lost money it had earned, not because the records were wrong, but because no one was watching for the letter.

Medicaid compliance is not just about filing correct claims. It is about maintaining documentation systems that survive an audit, and responding to audits correctly when they happen. A clinic that bills cleanly but manages its audit response poorly can lose as much as a clinic that bills incorrectly.

This module covers how Medicaid audits work, who conducts them, what they look for, and what the documentation coordinator's role is in both prevention and response. By the time you finish this module, you will know exactly what to do when a records request arrives, and what happens when nothing is done.

PART 1 OF CORE CONTENT
How Medicaid ABA audits work, the mechanics that documentation coordinators must understand.

Medicaid audits are not random inspections. They are systematic reviews of claims data that identify statistical anomalies, billing patterns, or specific providers for investigation. When a clinic's billing patterns fall outside expected norms, higher unit counts per session than peers, a high proportion of maximum-intensity sessions, geographic concentration of claims, the clinic may be selected for review. That review begins with a records request.

Prepayment vs post-payment audits

The most consequential distinction in Medicaid auditing is timing. A prepayment audit occurs before the claim is paid. The payer holds payment while it reviews documentation. If the documentation supports the claim, payment is released. If it does not, the claim is denied before any money changes hands. The financial impact of a prepayment audit denial is a lost payment, painful, but limited.

A post-payment audit occurs after the claim is paid. The payer has already issued payment. The audit reviews whether the documentation supported that payment. If it did not, the payer issues a recoupment demand, requiring the clinic to return money it has already received. For a clinic with 18 to 24 months of post-payment audit exposure, a large-scale finding can produce a recoupment demand that threatens the clinic's solvency. The Maine clinic lost $2.1 million this way. They had already spent some of that money running their operations.

How an audit begins

Most audits begin with a records request letter sent by mail and sometimes by email to the billing address on file with the payer. The letter identifies the claims under review (by date, CPT code, or service type), lists the documentation required for each claim, and states a response deadline, typically 30 to 45 days. Missing that deadline, for any reason, is treated as an inability to substantiate the claims. It is not an extension request. It is a default.

NON-NEGOTIABLE DEADLINE RULE

Audit response deadlines are real deadlines. They are not approximate dates. A response submitted one day late is a missed response. Build your clinic's internal protocol around receiving and processing records requests on the day they arrive, not when someone gets to them.

What happens after a findings letter

When an audit concludes with findings, documentation deficiencies, unsupported claims, or billing errors, the auditor issues a findings letter. The clinic typically has 30 to 60 days to respond with an appeal. Appealing effectively requires the same documentation that the original records request asked for, organized clearly, with a written response addressing each finding. The documentation coordinator's job in this phase is to support the appeal by locating, organizing, and verifying the accuracy of every document in the response package.

PART 2 OF CORE CONTENT
OIG, RAC, and MAC, the three audit entities and what each one means for your clinic.

Not all Medicaid auditors are the same entity. The documentation coordinator who understands which organization sent the records request understands the severity of the situation, the authority behind it, and the appropriate internal response. Here are the three primary audit entities in ABA Medicaid billing.

OIG, HIGHEST SEVERITY

Office of Inspector General

The federal oversight office responsible for detecting fraud, waste, and abuse in federal healthcare programs. An OIG audit is not routine. It indicates a specific concern has been elevated for investigation. OIG findings can result in exclusion from Medicaid and Medicare programs, civil monetary penalties, and referral for criminal prosecution. When the OIG contacts your clinic, the response involves legal counsel, not just documentation retrieval.

RAC, HIGH SEVERITY

Recovery Audit Contractor

Private contractors authorized by CMS to identify and recover improper Medicaid payments. RACs are paid a contingency fee based on overpayments recovered, which creates an incentive to find errors. RAC audits are common, and they target statistical outliers in billing patterns. A RAC finding results in a recoupment demand. Clinics have the right to appeal RAC findings through the Medicaid appeals process.

MAC, STANDARD

Medicaid Administrative Contractor

Medicare Administrative Contractors process claims and conduct routine audits as part of normal claims administration. MAC audits include prepayment reviews, medical review, and focused post-payment reviews. MAC audits are more routine and often narrower in scope than OIG or RAC reviews. A MAC finding typically results in a denial or a focused recoupment demand on the reviewed claims.

How to tell which entity sent the records request

The letter itself identifies the sender. The letterhead, the organization name, and the specific request language identify whether the contact is from the OIG, a RAC contractor, or a MAC. Any records request from an OIG office should trigger immediate escalation to the clinic director and legal counsel, not a documentation retrieval project. RAC and MAC requests follow the standard protocol this module covers.

The appeals process

Every Medicaid program includes an appeals process for contested audit findings. The specific steps vary by state, but the general structure is: redetermination (reviewed by the same contractor), reconsideration (reviewed by a Qualified Independent Contractor), ALJ hearing, and further escalation to the appeals board and federal courts. Each level has its own deadline. Missing any deadline can waive the right to appeal at that level. The documentation coordinator's job is to ensure the clinic's response packages are complete, accurate, and submitted on time at every level.

PART 3 OF CORE CONTENT
What ABA Medicaid auditors actually look for, the five most common findings.

OIG work plans, RAC audit findings, and state Medicaid program integrity reports consistently identify the same documentation deficiencies in ABA billing. The documentation coordinator who understands these five categories can prevent most audit findings through pre-submission review. Each one is preventable before the claim is filed.

1. Missing or incomplete session notes

The most common audit finding in ABA Medicaid billing is a session note that does not exist, cannot be located, or does not contain the required elements. Auditors look for: a note for every billed session, a note that documents the specific service billed (not a generic template entry), the client's response and data for the session, the specific targets addressed, the start and end times in units, and the supervising clinician identified by name and NPI.

2. Provider credential mismatch

Medicaid requires that the provider listed on the claim be credentialed with the payer for the service billed. If a clinic bills 97153 under a BCBA's NPI rather than the delivering RBT's NPI, or vice versa, the credential does not match the service. If the delivering technician's RBT credential was not on file with the payer at the time of service, the claim has no credentialed provider.

3. Authorization boundary violations

Services delivered outside the authorization boundaries, after the authorization expiration date, in excess of authorized units, in a location not covered by the authorization, or by a provider not listed in the authorization, are automatically non-covered regardless of the clinical quality of the service. Auditors cross-reference every session note against the active authorization for that date.

4. Medical necessity documentation gaps

Medicaid requires that ABA services be medically necessary, meaning they address a documented functional impairment, are delivered at the intensity that the treatment plan supports, and are not duplicating another service. Auditors look for treatment plans that justify the billed intensity and session notes that document progress (or the clinical rationale for continued service) against the treatment plan goals.

5. Time and unit discrepancies

As Module 3 established, session notes that record time in a format that does not match the billed units are documentation discrepancies. Auditors also look for sessions billed for more units than the session note supports (a note documenting 60 minutes but a claim for 8 units), and for sessions billed on dates where the note documents less time than what was claimed.

PART 4 OF CORE CONTENT
Responding to a Medicaid records request, the protocol that keeps a clinic whole.

The documentation coordinator's most critical compliance function is not writing notes, it is managing what happens when an audit begins. A clinic can have excellent documentation and still lose an audit by responding poorly. The following protocol applies to any Medicaid records request from a RAC or MAC. OIG requests require legal counsel involvement from step one.

The records request response protocol

DAY 1, RECEIPT
Log the letter's receipt date in a shared audit tracking document. Calculate the exact deadline (count calendar days from the date on the letter, not the date received). Identify every claim listed in the request and every document required. Escalate immediately to the clinic director and billing director. Do not wait.
DAYS 2–7, DOCUMENT INVENTORY
Pull every document requested: session notes, treatment plans, authorization approvals, supervisor credentials, and progress notes. Create a tracking grid: one row per claim, one column per required document. Mark each cell as present, missing, or deficient. Do not alter any document at this stage, inventory only.
DAYS 7–14, INTERNAL REVIEW
Present the tracking grid to the compliance team. For missing documents: determine if they exist and can be located, or if they were never created. For deficient documents: escalate to the clinical team to determine whether an addendum is appropriate and clinically defensible. Never alter a note to fix a deficiency without clinical team authorization and a proper addendum process.
DAYS 14–28, ASSEMBLY
Assemble the response package: organized by claim, with each required document clearly labeled and cross-referenced to the claim it supports. Include a cover letter that identifies the clinic, the audit reference number, the claims being responded to, and a table of contents. Keep a copy of everything submitted.
BY DAY 30, SUBMISSION
Submit 5 to 7 days before the deadline, not on deadline day. Use the submission method specified in the records request (certified mail, secure portal, or fax with confirmation). Get a submission confirmation and retain it. The deadline date is the last acceptable day, not the target day.

What must never happen in audit response

  • Never alter a session note to fix a deficiency. Altering a note after a records request has been received, even to correct a genuine error, is evidence alteration. This converts a documentation deficiency into potential fraud. Any note change must go through the proper addendum process with a date stamp and the clinician's signature.
  • Never submit incomplete documentation without noting what is missing. A response package that omits requested documents without explanation is treated as a failure to substantiate those claims. If documents are missing, the response letter must acknowledge the gap and explain why.
  • Never ignore a records request. The Maine clinic lost $2.1 million this way. There is no outcome worse than missing a deadline.

PART 5 OF CORE CONTENT
Building an audit-ready documentation system, the structure that makes response possible.

An audit-ready clinic is not one that never gets audited. It is one that can respond to any records request within the timeframe required because its documentation system is organized, complete, and retrievable. Building that system is an ongoing function of the documentation coordinator, not a project that happens after a records request arrives.

The five components of an audit-ready documentation system

Component What it is Why it matters in an audit Priority
Documentation policy Written standards for every required element of each CPT code, by provider type and payer Demonstrates that the clinic has an established standard, auditors can see the standard the clinic holds itself to Critical
Client file structure One file per client containing: referral, diagnosis, treatment plan, authorization approvals, all session notes, progress notes, and discharge summary Allows any session note to be pulled and cross-referenced to its authorization within minutes, not hours Critical
Provider credential log Current credentials for every billing provider: RBT certificates, BCBA licenses, NPI numbers, and payer credentialing status Auditors verify that every provider was credentialed with the payer at the time of service, the log is what you produce as evidence Critical
Authorization tracking Active authorization records per client, per payer, with approved units, expiration dates, and approved providers Auditors cross-reference every session against the authorization in effect on that date, your tracking must show the authorization was active High
Pre-submission QA process A defined documentation review step that happens before claims are submitted, checking for the five common deficiency categories from Part 3 The documentation coordinator who catches errors before submission creates no audit exposure, the auditor sees clean documentation High

The 24-hour retrieval standard

A well-designed documentation system allows any session note, authorization approval, or provider credential to be retrieved within 24 hours of a records request. If your clinic cannot retrieve a specific session note for a specific client on a specific date in that timeframe, your documentation system is not audit-ready. The 24-hour standard is not a regulatory requirement, it is a practical threshold that keeps audit response manageable even on compressed timelines.

Ongoing quality review

An audit-ready system does not wait for audit exposure to find deficiencies. The documentation coordinator's ongoing quality review function should include: weekly spot checks of session notes against the documentation policy, monthly cross-referencing of billed units against authorization remaining, quarterly review of provider credentialing status, and an annual documentation policy review against updated payer billing guidelines.

WORKED EXAMPLE
A records request arrives. Walk through the first 72 hours.

The following scenario walks through how a documentation coordinator at an audit-ready clinic handles the first 72 hours after a RAC records request arrives. Each action is annotated with the reason it matters.

The situation

Tuesday, 9:14 AM. A certified mail envelope arrives at the clinic. The documentation coordinator signs for it. It is a Medicaid RAC audit records request for 35 claims filed between January and June of this year. The letter is dated four days ago. The deadline is 45 days from the date of the letter, which means the effective response window is 41 days from today.

9:14 AM, RECEIPT
Log the receipt date and the letter date in the audit tracking log. Calculate the deadline: 45 days from the letter date. Mark "effective days remaining" as 41. Send a same-day email to the clinic director and billing director with the records request attached and the deadline highlighted.
DAY 1 AFTERNOON, CLAIM INVENTORY
Build a tracking grid in a spreadsheet: one row per claim (35 rows), columns for session note, treatment plan, authorization approval, and provider credential. Begin pulling each document from the client file system. By end of day 1, the grid shows: 33 of 35 session notes located; 2 missing; all 35 authorization approvals present; 1 provider credential expiring next month (not an audit issue, flagged for credential renewal action).
DAY 2, ESCALATION MEETING
Present the tracking grid to the clinical and billing teams. The 2 missing session notes are escalated: the clinical director confirms both sessions occurred and believes the notes were filed in PrioraCare but not exported. PrioraCare export is initiated. Notes are located and exported by end of day 2. No documentation has been altered, notes are as originally recorded.
DAYS 3–28, REVIEW AND ASSEMBLY
All 35 session notes are reviewed against the documentation policy checklist. 3 notes have supervisor listed as "Dr. M" without NPI. Clinical director adds a contemporaneous addendum to each note with the full name and NPI, properly dated and signed. Final tracking grid: 35 of 35 session notes complete, 35 of 35 authorizations present, all provider credentials verified. Response package assembled.
DAY 35, SUBMISSION
Response package submitted via the RAC's secure portal with a cover letter, table of contents, and certification from the clinic director. Submission confirmation retained. Deadline was day 41. Submitted 6 days early.
WHAT MADE THIS RESPONSE WORK

Four things separated this clinic from the Maine clinic.

First: the records request was identified and logged the day it arrived. Second: the documentation system allowed retrieval of 33 of 35 notes on day one. Third: the 2 missing notes were located in PrioraCare, they existed in the EHR, they were not missing from the clinical record. Fourth: the 3 supervisor documentation issues were resolved through a proper addendum process, not by altering original notes.

An audit-ready clinic does not need every note to be perfect at the time of the records request. It needs a system that can find, review, and correct (through proper addendum) documentation issues within the response window.

MODULE SCENARIO
The letter has been sitting unopened for nine days. What do you do now?
YOUR ROLE: DOCUMENTATION COORDINATOR

The Setup

It is a Thursday. The clinic's billing coordinator, Jared, has been out sick for two weeks. His desk has accumulated mail. The office manager asks you to clear his desk. You find a certified mail envelope, opened but unread. Inside is a Medicaid RAC records request dated 11 days ago. The deadline is 45 days from the letter date, meaning you have 34 days left. The request covers 42 claims across 8 clients.

Your first instinct is to panic. Work through three decisions instead.

DECISION POINT 1 OF 3
You have 34 days. The first action you take is:
A. Call the RAC to explain that the letter was missed and request a 30-day extension.
B. Begin pulling session notes immediately so you can start assembling the response package today.
C. Log the letter date and the receipt date, calculate the exact deadline, and immediately escalate to the clinic director and billing director with the full letter attached.
DECISION POINT 2 OF 3
The clinic director asks whether you should call the RAC to explain the delay and request more time. What do you advise?
A. Yes, explaining the billing coordinator's absence should earn goodwill and a formal extension.
B. No, the 34 days remaining is a workable window. Calling the RAC draws attention to the delay without guaranteed benefit. Focus resources on the response, not the extension request. If the timeline becomes impossible due to document retrieval issues, then contact the RAC.
C. No, contacting the RAC about an ongoing audit is prohibited under Medicaid program rules.
DECISION POINT 3 OF 3
On day 20 of your review, you discover that 6 of the 42 session notes contain a known deficiency, supervisor listed as "clinic BCBA" rather than by name and NPI. The clinical team confirms which BCBA supervised each session. What is the correct documentation action?
A. Update the original session notes to add the BCBA name and NPI with today's date as the edit date.
B. Have the supervising BCBA for each session add a properly dated and signed addendum to each note, identifying themselves by name and NPI and stating the addendum's purpose, without altering the original note content.
C. Submit the 6 deficient notes without addressing the issue and address the finding if the RAC raises it.
JOB AID
Pre-Submission Audit Checklist

Use this checklist before submitting any weekly claims batch to Medicaid payers. The documentation coordinator who catches these issues before submission creates zero audit exposure. The documentation coordinator who catches them after a records request arrives is managing a crisis.

CADA Pre-Submission Audit Checklist

CareGuard · cadahealth.org · Weekly claims review before submission

SESSION NOTE COMPLETENESS
1
Note exists for every billed session. Every CPT code on this week's claim batch has a session note in the client file. No claim goes out without a note.
2
Time recorded in units, not hours. Every note documents start time, end time, and total units. Notes saying "2 hours" or "1.5 hrs" are returned to the clinical team before the claim is submitted.
3
Units on note match units on claim. Compare the units in the session note to the units in the billing system for every claim line. Any discrepancy is a stop, do not submit until resolved.
4
Specific targets are documented. The note identifies the ABA programs or behavioral targets addressed in the session, not "continued ABA therapy" or "skills training." The specific target names appear.
5
Client response and data are present. The note documents the client's response to the session and includes trial data or observational data for each target addressed.
PROVIDER AND AUTHORIZATION VERIFICATION
6
Delivering provider is identified by name and NPI. The session note names the person who delivered the service. For 97153: the RBT's name and the supervising BCBA's name and NPI. For 97155: the BCBA's name and NPI.
7
Provider is credentialed with the payer for the service date. Cross-reference the credential log: the delivering provider's credential was active and on file with this payer on the session date. Expired credentials are a stop.
8
Authorization covers this session. The session date falls within the authorization period, the service location matches the authorization, and the remaining authorized units cover the units being billed.
9
CPT code matches the documented service and the delivering provider type. A technician-delivered note bills under a technician code. A BCBA note documenting protocol modification bills under 97155. Code and note match.
MODIFIER AND CONCURRENT BILLING CHECK
10
Required modifiers are present. Compare each claim line's modifiers to this payer's billing guidelines. Required modifiers (HM, HN, HO, GT, U-codes) are attached. No modifier is attached without a basis in the payer's guidelines.
11
No prohibited concurrent billing pairs. Review this payer's billing guidelines for concurrent billing restrictions. Check this week's claims for any client billed for two codes during overlapping time windows that the payer prohibits.
If any item fails: Do not submit that claim. Flag it, return it to the clinical or billing team for correction, document the issue and the resolution, and recheck before resubmitting. A claim held for one week costs one week's cash flow. A claim submitted with a deficiency and found in a post-payment audit costs one week's payment plus recoupment demand plus audit defense costs.
